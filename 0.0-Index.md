## 1. Project name

**iRead**

An EPUB reading app for iPad that lets readers skim descriptive passages without losing track of who or what the story is talking about.

---

## 2. Background

### Who it is for

The primary users are **fiction readers with aphantasia**, people who cannot form mental images. Two groups matter:

- **Hobby readers** who already read fiction regularly.
- **New readers** who want to start reading fiction but find it hard to follow.

### The problem

Readers with aphantasia tend to **skim long descriptive passages**: scenery, character appearance, and world building. How much they skim depends on how the author tells the story.

The trouble comes later. Authors often refer back to a person or thing by appearance instead of by name, for example *"the red short hair girl"* or *"the rusty door"*. A reader who skimmed the original description no longer knows who or what is meant.

### What readers do today

When a phrase confuses them, readers scroll or flip back and re-read earlier pages, one by one, until they find the sentence that first introduced that person or thing. This is:

- **Time-consuming**, because there is no shortcut to the right spot.
- **Disruptive**, because it breaks the flow of reading.
- **Risky with search**, because in-book search needs the exact words and also shows later matches that can spoil the story.

### What iRead does

The reader **taps an underlined phrase** (a character, setting, or event that is referred to by description). On the same page, iRead shows a card with the author's own passage that gives the **best context** for the tapped phrase: usually the **First Mention**, but a later passage when it explains the phrase better (the **context passage**). The reader can jump to that passage and jump back.

iRead **does not generate an answer**. It points the reader to the paragraph the author wrote. It only uses text the reader has **already read**, so it cannot spoil what comes next. This also holds for story updates: if a later passage, even the next page, changes or reveals something about a character, setting, or event, the card does not show it until the reader gets there (PRD 2.1 §5.4).

### Why Apple

The goal is to build fully on the Apple ecosystem and use what the reader's own device can do: on-device language processing (Natural Language framework and Apple's on-device foundation model), so book content stays on the device.

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **First Mention** | The earlier sentence where the author **first gave the description** that the reader tapped, which identifies the same person or thing. It is not simply the person's first appearance. Linguists call this the *antecedent*; the product uses "First Mention". See the example below. |
| **Context passage** | The passage shown on the card: among the author's sentences **at or before the tapped phrase**, the one that gives the best context for **that phrase**. It is the First Mention unless a later sentence explains the phrase better, for example "Mira, the village doctor" when the reader taps "the doctor". See PRD 2.1 §5.1. |
| **Descriptive reference** | A phrase that refers to a person or thing by appearance or description instead of by name, e.g. "the red short hair girl". |
| **Current position** | Where the reader is in the book. Nothing after this point, or after the furthest sentence the reader has seen (the **reading frontier**, PRD 1.3), is ever used. |

**First Mention example (definition of done)**

- Page 3: *"Mira arrived at the station."* (first appearance, no description)
- Page 5: *"Mira, a girl with short red hair, pushed through the crowd."* (description first given)
- Page 40: *"The red short hair girl smiled."* (reader taps "red short hair girl")

Correct result: the **page 5** sentence, because that is where the author first described the girl this way. Page 3 is not the First Mention, because it does not contain the description.

**Context passage example**

- Page 5: *"Mira, a girl with short red hair, pushed through the crowd."*
- Page 12: *"Mira, the village doctor, ran to the barn."*
- Page 40: *"The doctor frowned."* (reader taps "The doctor")

Correct result: the **page 12** sentence, because it links "doctor" to Mira. Tapping "the red short hair girl" on the same page still gives page 5.

## 3. Success metrics

| # | Metric | Target | How it is measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Accuracy** | The passage shown is the correct context passage (First Mention, or a later passage that explains the tapped phrase better; called *context passage accuracy* in PRD 2.1 §4.3): at least **90%** for Character and Setting, **80%** for Event. **[Proposed]** | English golden set of public-domain novels, annotated by 2 annotators for all three entity types (PRD 2.1 §4.3). Number of books: TBD. **[Proposed]** |
| 2 | **No spoilers** | **0** results, and **0** story updates, taken from text after the reading boundary. This is a hard requirement, not a target. | Automated check on the golden set: every name, description, relation, involvement, and update on a card must be positioned at or before the boundary (PRD 2.1 §4.3, §5.4). |
| 3 | **Readers keep reading** | Readers with aphantasia complete **more chapters** with iRead than without it. | **Pilot phase, after the demo.** The app counts chapters finished **on the device**; testers choose to share their stats through an in-app export, plus a short survey. The feature can be switched off per tester to compare with and without (e.g. 1 week each). **[Proposed]** |

Speed compared to flipping back manually is part of the problem statement but is not a headline metric.

### How iRead is different

Kindle and Google Play Books use AI to **answer the question with text the AI writes**. iRead **does not write an answer**; it **takes the reader to the paragraph the author wrote** that resolves the confusion, then lets them jump back.

What follows from that **[Proposed]**:

- **No made-up facts.** The result is always the author's own sentence.
- **One tap, one result.** No question to type.
- **Any EPUB the reader imports**, not tied to one store, country, or title list.
- **On-device.** Book content is processed on the iPad, not sent to a server.
- **Built for aphantasic readers** from the start.

## 6. Constraints and decisions

| Decision | Detail |
|---|---|
| Platform | iPadOS native, Swift, SwiftUI, MVVM. **iPad only for this development.** iPhone (iOS) and macOS are **TBD** and need their own decision before any work starts. |
| First release | **Demoable app** on iPad, matching the "Demoable app" release target of PRD 1.0 to 2.1. The community pilot through TestFlight comes **after the demo**. **[Proposed]** No onboarding polish, monetisation, or App Store review needed yet. |
| Pilot measurement (after the demo) | Not part of the demo scope: the chapter-finished event and the per-tester on/off switch for Reference Passage are added when the pilot is planned. No analytics server. Reading stats stay on the device until the tester chooses to export and share them. Reference Passage has an on/off switch for the with/without comparison. **[Proposed]** |
| Minimum OS | iPadOS 26. **[Proposed]** |
| Device requirement | Character and Setting references work with the NaturalLanguage framework on any supported iPad. Events first narrated through verbs, and ambiguous phrases, need Apple Intelligence (Foundation Models); without it they are simply not underlined (PRD 2.1 A4). Import, Library, and Reader work on any supported device. **[Proposed]** |
| On-device processing | Book content is processed on the device using Apple's frameworks. "Built on the Apple ecosystem" covers AI processing and data; it does not rule out third-party libraries. |
| EPUB parsing and rendering | **Built in-house** (decided): own EPUB parser (PRD 1.1) and own paginator (PRD 1.3). Epic 2 needs sentence-level positions, tap targets, and underlines on the exact text, so the app keeps full control instead of mapping positions through a third-party toolkit. A toolkit such as Readium can be reconsidered later. |
| Book language | **English** books only for the demo and the pilot. Test novels for the accuracy metric are English. |
| Supported files | DRM-free EPUB files only. Store-bought EPUBs are usually copy-protected, and Kindle books are not EPUB. Where readers get DRM-free books (e.g. public-domain catalogues) is left to PRD 1.1. **[Proposed]** |
| No spoilers | The limit is the **reading boundary**: the later of the tapped phrase's position and the reading frontier (the furthest sentence the reader has seen). Only information at or before it is shown, **including story updates**: a later passage that changes or reveals something about a character, setting, or event does not show until the reader reaches it (PRD 2.1 §5.4). Jumping back to a First Mention or re-reading does not move the limit. **[Proposed]** |

### Known technical risks

- **In-house pagination.** Building the paginator and EPUB parser ourselves takes more work than using a toolkit and must handle varied EPUB markup, font size changes, and iPad rotation and Split View / Stage Manager (PRD 1.1 §8, PRD 1.3 §5.4).
- Apple's Natural Language framework recognises names of people, places, and organisations, but has **no coreference resolution** (linking "the red short hair girl" back to her introduction). This part must be built on top of Apple's tools.
- Apple's on-device foundation model has a small context window (about **4,096 tokens**, input and output combined, on current models; it can differ per OS version, so PRD 2.1 §5.3 B reads it at runtime), so a whole book cannot be passed in. Candidate sentences must be narrowed down first.
- A developer forum report says Apple's English sentence embedding returns nil on some physical iOS 27 devices; worth testing early, including on iPadOS 27. PRD 2.1 §5.6 uses embeddings for ranking only and falls back to rules when they are unavailable.
